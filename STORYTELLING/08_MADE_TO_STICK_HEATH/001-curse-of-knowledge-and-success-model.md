# The Curse of Knowledge & the SUCCESs Model

## The Core Question

A false rumor about a company travels the whole office by lunch and is still repeated a year later. A
carefully-researched, genuinely important safety memo is forgotten by everyone before they reach the
parking lot. The rumor is *worse* by every measure — untrue, unimportant — yet it *sticks* and the
memo doesn't. **Why do some ideas lodge permanently in human memory with zero effort while better,
truer, more important ideas slide straight off — and can you deliberately engineer an idea to stick?**

## The Origin Story (history & the thinking behind it)

The book comes from a pair of brothers with complementary vantage points. **Chip Heath** is a
professor (organizational behavior at Stanford) who studied *why ideas spread* — including the
mechanics of urban legends and rumors, which are, in effect, ideas that have been ruthlessly optimized
for stickiness by generations of retelling. **Dan Heath** worked in education and idea-development. In
**2007** they published **_Made to Stick: Why Some Ideas Survive and Others Die_.** Their method was
reverse-engineering: study the ideas that *do* stick without anyone trying — proverbs ("a bird in the
hand..."), urban legends (the "kidney heist"), conspiracy theories, enduring ad slogans — and extract
the common traits. Naturally sticky ideas, they found, share a recurring recipe. Name the recipe, and
you can cook stickiness on purpose.

The book's most important single idea, though, isn't one of the six traits — it's the *villain* that
kills communication, and it comes from a beautiful piece of research. In **1990**, a Stanford graduate
student named **Elizabeth Newton** ran an experiment for her PhD. She split people into "tappers" and
"listeners." Each tapper picked a famous, universally-known song ("Happy Birthday," a national anthem)
and tapped its rhythm on a table; the listener tried to name the song. Before the listeners guessed,
the *tappers* were asked to predict what fraction of listeners would get it. Tappers predicted about
**50%**. The actual success rate was about **2.5%** — roughly 3 songs identified out of 120.

Why the staggering gap? Because inside the tapper's head, the full song is *playing* — melody, lyrics,
orchestration. They literally cannot imagine what it's like to hear only disconnected knocks on a
table, because they can no longer *not know* the song. That is the **Curse of Knowledge**: once you
know something, you lose the ability to imagine not knowing it, and this makes you a terrible
communicator to people who don't yet know it. The expert taps out their knowledge and hears a symphony;
the audience hears random tapping. The Heaths argue this is *the* central obstacle to communication —
and every one of their six principles is, at bottom, a technique for beating the Curse of Knowledge.

Placed in this course, the Heaths are storytelling pointed at *non-fiction communication* — teaching,
pitching, leading, explaining. And their sixth principle delivers the punchline that ties them back to
everything before: the reason **Stories** are the last and in some ways the master principle is that a
well-told story naturally embodies the other five, and it makes an idea both *memorable* (simulation)
and *actionable* (inspiration). Story isn't one tactic among six; it's the vehicle the other five ride
in.

## Why it exists (the pain it solves)

The pain is universal and, for you specifically, professional: **you understand something clearly and
completely fail to make anyone else understand it.** Engineers explaining architecture, founders
pitching, teachers teaching, anyone writing a doc — all constantly transmit *accurate* information that
doesn't stick, doesn't persuade, and doesn't drive action. The Heaths' diagnosis is that the problem is
almost never insufficient information (usually there's *too much*) — it's the **Curse of Knowledge**
plus a failure to shape the message for the human memory it's aimed at.

The SUCCESs model is the concrete fix: a six-item checklist that names *exactly* what's missing when a
message doesn't land. "My explanation isn't landing" is a fog; "my explanation is abstract, not
concrete, and has no story" is a to-do list. It converts the vague art of "communicate better" into a
diagnosable, fixable engineering problem.

## What it is

**Precise definition.** A "sticky" idea is one that is *understood, remembered, and changes behavior.*
The Heaths' claim is that sticky ideas reliably share six traits, spelling **SUCCESs**:

```
S — SIMPLE      Find the core. Strip to the single most important thing, then say it compactly.
                (Not "dumbed down" — prioritized: the one commander's-intent idea.)
U — UNEXPECTED  Break a pattern to grab attention, then open a curiosity GAP to hold it.
                Surprise gets attention; a gap in knowledge sustains it.
C — CONCRETE    Say it in terms of specific people, objects, and actions the senses can grasp —
                not abstractions. Concreteness is how ideas mean the same thing to everyone.
C — CREDIBLE    Make the idea believable — via authorities, vivid telling details, statistics
                that are made human, or a "try it yourself" test the audience can run.
E — EMOTIONAL   Make people FEEL something. We're wired to feel for individuals, not abstractions
                or statistics. Feeling is what makes us care enough to act.
S — STORY       Wrap it in a story. Stories are simulation (they teach how to act) + inspiration
                (they give the energy to act). Story carries the other five.
```

(The final lowercase "s" is just there to make the acronym pronounceable — there are six principles.)

**Mental model.** SUCCESs is a **six-point pre-flight checklist for any idea before it leaves your
head.** A pilot doesn't trust memory to catch a missing step; they run the list. Likewise, before you
send the doc or give the pitch, you run: Simple? Unexpected? Concrete? Credible? Emotional? Story? Each
"no" is a specific reason the idea will crash on landing. The checklist doesn't make you creative — it
catches the predictable failures that the Curse of Knowledge blinds you to.

**Real-world analogy.** Think of an idea as **cargo you need to ship across a river into someone else's
mind.** Raw abstract information is loose sand — it slips through your fingers and washes away
mid-crossing (the forgotten memo). The six principles are how you pack the cargo so it survives: Simple
= don't send the whole warehouse, send the one crate that matters; Concrete = pack it in a solid box,
not loose; Emotional/Unexpected = label it so the receiver actually *wants* to carry it inland; and
Story = the *boat* that carries all the crates across at once. The naturally-sticky ideas (proverbs,
legends) are cargo that got packed this way by accident over centuries; you're going to do it on
purpose.

## How it works (under the hood)

The deep structure is: **every principle is a specific antidote to the Curse of Knowledge**, and
**Story is the container that administers all of them at once.** Walk it.

```
THE VILLAIN: Curse of Knowledge — you can't imagine not knowing what you know,
so you communicate in abstractions, skip the core, and forget to make people feel.

   ANTIDOTE            HOW IT DEFEATS THE CURSE
   ─────────           ────────────────────────────────────────────────
   SIMPLE          →   forces you to choose the ONE core idea the novice needs,
                       instead of dumping everything you know at once.
   UNEXPECTED      →   you assume the audience is as interested as you are (they're not);
                       surprise + a curiosity gap manufacture the interest you take for granted.
   CONCRETE        →   experts drift to abstraction ("scalability", "synergy"); concreteness
                       drags the idea back to things a novice's senses can actually hold.
   CREDIBLE        →   you forget the audience hasn't earned your certainty; you supply
                       proof they can check, rather than expecting them to just trust you.
   EMOTIONAL       →   you're excited by the abstract principle; the audience needs to feel
                       it about a specific person to care. You make them feel.
   STORY           →   the ultimate cure: a story forces the concrete, the emotional, the
                       specific — you literally cannot tell a good story in pure abstraction.
```

Two mechanisms deserve a closer look because they're the highest-leverage.

**The curiosity gap (inside "Unexpected").** The Heaths distinguish *getting* attention (surprise —
break a pattern) from *holding* it (open a gap). Their model of interest: point out a specific gap in
the audience's knowledge — a question they now realize they can't answer — and the discomfort of that
gap pulls them toward the answer. You don't open with "here are the facts about our sales"; you open
with "we lost our biggest customer last quarter, and the reason will surprise you." The gap is now
open; the brain itches to close it. (This is Dicks' "open questions" and Cron's prediction-engine, in
a business suit.)

**Emotion via the individual, not the aggregate.** The Heaths cite research on charitable giving
showing people give far more generously to *one named, pictured child* than to statistics about
millions in need — the abstraction of large numbers actually *dampens* feeling. The mechanism: humans
are wired to feel for individuals, not for aggregates ("the death of one is a tragedy; the death of a
million is a statistic," as the grim saying goes). So to make an idea emotional, you attach it to a
single concrete person the audience can care about — which is, not coincidentally, exactly what a story
does.

**Why Story is the master principle — simulation + inspiration.** The Heaths make a precise claim
about *why* stories drive action. A story does two things at once:

```
SIMULATION   → a story is a mental rehearsal; hearing how someone solved a problem is
               like practicing the solution yourself. It teaches HOW to act. (Cron's
               survival-simulator, restated for business: stories are practice flights.)
INSPIRATION  → a story of someone overcoming makes you FEEL you can too; it supplies the
               MOTIVATION to act, which raw instruction never does.
```

Knowledge tells you *what*; a story makes the *what* both memorable (because you simulated it) and
motivating (because you felt it). That's why the Heaths put Story last: it's the vehicle that, when you
tell it well, automatically smuggles in Simple (a story has a clear point), Concrete (real people and
objects), Emotional (you feel for the character), Unexpected (the turn), and often Credible (it
happened). The other five are the payload; the story is the truck.

**A worked contrast — the same idea, cursed vs sticky:**

```
CURSED (abstract, expert-brain):
  "We need to improve our incident response times by optimizing cross-functional
   escalation pathways and reducing mean-time-to-acknowledge."
   → true, forgotten in 30 seconds. Loose sand.

STICKY (SUCCESs applied):
  SIMPLE:      "When the site goes down, minutes cost us customers."
  UNEXPECTED:  "Last month our biggest client's checkout was broken for 47 minutes —
                and we didn't even know until THEY called us." (opens a gap: how?)
  CONCRETE:    "The alert fired at 2:03. It sat unread in a channel nobody watches until 2:50."
  EMOTIONAL:   "Priya, their engineer, spent her evening apologizing to HER boss for OUR outage."
  CREDIBLE:    "Here's the timeline from the logs — check it yourself."
  STORY:       the whole thing, told as Priya's bad night, ending on the fix we're proposing.
   → remembered for a year, and it drives the behavior change. The boat made it across.
```

Same information. One is a memo nobody finishes; the other is a story people repeat — which is exactly
the rumor-vs-memo asymmetry the book set out to solve, now flipped in *your* favor.

## How to implement it

Model SUCCESs as a literal checklist and let it flag what's missing — because the Curse of Knowledge
means *you cannot reliably feel the gaps yourself.*

```typescript
interface Idea {
  core: string;            // the ONE thing (Simple)
  patternBreak: string;    // the surprise (Unexpected: get attention)
  curiosityGap: string;    // the open question (Unexpected: hold attention)
  concreteImage: string;   // specific people/objects/actions (Concrete)
  proof: string;           // checkable credibility (Credible)
  feltIndividual: string;  // the ONE person we're made to feel for (Emotional)
  story: string;           // the narrative wrapping it all (Story)
}

function stickinessCheck(i: Idea): string[] {
  const missing: string[] = [];
  if (!i.core || i.core.split(" ").length > 15) missing.push("SIMPLE: no compact single core idea.");
  if (!i.patternBreak) missing.push("UNEXPECTED: nothing breaks the pattern to grab attention.");
  if (!i.curiosityGap) missing.push("UNEXPECTED: no curiosity gap to hold attention.");
  if (!i.concreteImage) missing.push("CONCRETE: still abstract — give it senses (people, objects, actions).");
  if (!i.proof) missing.push("CREDIBLE: give proof the audience can check.");
  if (!i.feltIndividual) missing.push("EMOTIONAL: attach it to ONE person — we don't feel for aggregates.");
  if (!i.story) missing.push("STORY: wrap it in a narrative — the vehicle that carries the other five.");
  return missing.length ? missing : ["Sticky: survives the Curse of Knowledge on all six axes."];
}
```

The value is exactly that it's mechanical. Your expert brain *hears the symphony* and will swear the
tapping is obvious; the checklist doesn't have the Curse, so it catches the missing crate you're blind
to. Run any important message through it before sending. The most common flags for technical people are
**CONCRETE** (you defaulted to abstraction) and **EMOTIONAL/STORY** (you gave data, not a person). Fix
those two and most "why isn't this landing?" problems dissolve.

## Trade-offs, when to use, and pitfalls

**When to reach for SUCCESs.** Any time you need an idea to be *understood, remembered, and acted on*
by someone who doesn't already share your context: pitches, docs, teaching, onboarding, presentations,
change management, marketing, even naming things. It's the most directly job-applicable framework in
the course — and it's a *diagnostic*, so reach for it whenever communication fails and you don't know
why.

**When to be careful.** The framework is for *communicating* ideas, not *validating* them — a false
idea packed with SUCCESs sticks just as well (that's literally how rumors and propaganda work), so the
model carries an ethical edge: stickiness is a power that's indifferent to truth. Use it to make *true*
things land, and stay aware that the same tools weaponize misinformation. Also, don't force all six
onto every trivial message — a two-line Slack update doesn't need a story arc. Reserve the full
checklist for ideas that genuinely *must* stick.

**Common pitfalls:**

- **Forgetting you have the Curse of Knowledge.** The meta-pitfall. You will feel your message is
  clear because the symphony is playing in *your* head. Assume you're cursed and use the checklist —
  don't trust your own sense of clarity.
- **Confusing "simple" with "dumbed down."** Simple means *finding and leading with the core*, not
  removing nuance or talking down. It's ruthless prioritization, not stupidity.
- **Statistics instead of a person.** Leading with aggregate numbers to prove importance. Numbers
  inform but don't move; attach the idea to one concrete individual to make it *felt*, then use data
  to back it up.
- **Surprise without a gap.** A shocking opener that grabs attention but opens no curiosity gap — you
  get the glance and then lose them. Surprise must hand off to a question that pulls them forward.
- **Data dump over story.** Presenting complete information and trusting it to persuade. Information is
  the payload; without the story-vehicle (and the emotional/concrete packing), it doesn't cross the
  river. Lead with the story; let the data support it.

## 🧠 Q&A Bank

**1. What is the Curse of Knowledge, and what experiment demonstrates it?**
It's the inability, once you know something, to imagine not knowing it — which makes experts poor
communicators to novices. Elizabeth Newton's 1990 "tappers and listeners" study demonstrates it:
tappers of famous songs predicted ~50% would be identified from the taps; only ~2.5% were, because the
tapper hears the full song in their head while the listener hears only knocks.

**2. What does SUCCESs stand for, and what does each principle do?**
Simple (find and lead with the single core), Unexpected (break a pattern to get attention, open a
curiosity gap to hold it), Concrete (specific people/objects/actions, not abstractions), Credible
(believable via authority, vivid detail, or a testable claim), Emotional (make people feel — for an
individual, not an aggregate), Stories (wrap it in a narrative that carries the other five). The final
"s" just makes the acronym pronounceable.

**3. Why is Story the "master" principle rather than just one of six?**
Because a well-told story naturally embodies the other five — it's concrete, emotional, has a clear
point (simple), a turn (unexpected), and often really happened (credible). And it does two things raw
information can't: *simulation* (mental rehearsal of how to act) and *inspiration* (the motivation to
act). Story is the vehicle the other five ride in.

**4. What's the difference between getting attention and holding it, in the "Unexpected" principle?**
Getting attention comes from *surprise* — breaking a pattern or expectation. Holding attention comes
from opening a *curiosity gap* — pointing to a specific hole in the audience's knowledge (a question
they now can't answer) so the discomfort pulls them toward the answer. Surprise without a gap gets a
glance and then loses them.

**5. Why do the Heaths say to make an idea emotional via one individual rather than statistics?**
Because humans are wired to feel for concrete individuals, not aggregates — research on giving shows
people are far more generous to one named, pictured person than to statistics about millions, and big
numbers actually *dampen* feeling. To make an idea move people, attach it to a single person they can
care about, then use data to support (not replace) that feeling.

**6. "What happens if…" — a technically accurate, complete, well-organized memo is forgotten
instantly. What's the likely SUCCESs diagnosis?**
Usually it's Concrete (it's abstract — full of terms like "optimize" and "scalability" with no
sensory anchor) and Emotional/Story (it's information with no person to feel for and no narrative
vehicle). It probably also isn't Simple (too much, no single core). The information is loose sand;
without concreteness, emotion, and a story to carry it, it washes out before it sticks.

**7. What's the ethical edge of the SUCCESs model?**
Stickiness is indifferent to truth — the model was partly reverse-engineered from rumors, urban
legends, and propaganda, which stick *because* they're SUCCESs-shaped. So the same tools that make a
true idea land also make a false one spread. The framework is for *communicating* ideas, not
*validating* them; use it to make true things stick, and be aware it can weaponize misinformation.

**8. How does Made to Stick connect to Cron's *Wired for Story*?**
Directly: the Heaths' claim that stories drive action through *simulation* (mental rehearsal) is Cron's
survival-simulator idea applied to communication, and their "Emotional via an individual" reflects
Cron's point that emotion assigns meaning. Both say the brain is built to learn from felt, concrete,
individual experience — which is what a story delivers and abstraction doesn't.

## 🛠️ Hands-on Exercise

Take a real idea you've recently failed to get across — an architecture decision, a process change, a
pitch that fell flat. Run it through the SUCCESs checklist and honestly mark which of the six it was
missing (be suspicious of "it had them all" — that's the Curse of Knowledge talking). Then rebuild it:
write the **one core sentence** (Simple), open it with a **curiosity gap** (Unexpected), anchor it to
**one named person** and what they concretely experienced (Concrete + Emotional), add **checkable
proof** (Credible), and tell the whole thing as a 60-second **story**. Deliver both versions to a
colleague a day apart and see which they remember. Hint: for technical people the missing crates are
almost always Concrete and Story — you gave the accurate abstraction and trusted it to carry itself.

## ✅ Recap

- The **Curse of Knowledge** — once you know something you can't imagine not knowing it — is the
  central villain of communication (proven by the "tappers and listeners" study: ~50% predicted vs
  ~2.5% actual). You are cursed; assume it.
- Sticky ideas share six traits — **SUCCESs**: Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional,
  Story — and every one is an antidote to the Curse.
- **Unexpected** = surprise (get attention) + curiosity gap (hold attention). **Emotional** = attach
  the idea to **one individual**, because we don't feel for aggregates.
- **Story is the master principle**: it carries the other five and drives action through **simulation**
  (rehearsal of how to act) + **inspiration** (motivation to act). Lead with the story; let data
  support it.
- Stickiness is **indifferent to truth** — use it to make true things land, and know it's the same
  engine behind rumor and propaganda.
