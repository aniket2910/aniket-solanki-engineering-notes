# Telling Them Well — Stakes, Elephant, Backpack, Crystal Ball

## The Core Question

Lesson 001 solved *finding* a story: you now know your life is full of five-second moments of change.
But finding a story and *holding a room with it* are different skills. You've felt this: someone has
genuinely interesting material and still loses the table — people's eyes drift, someone checks their
phone. The material was fine; the *telling* leaked tension. So: **once you have a true story, what
are the concrete devices that create suspense and emotion — that make an audience physically lean in
and stay leaned in — even when nothing is exploding?**

## The Origin Story (history & the thinking behind it)

Return to the Moth StorySLAM constraints from lesson 001: five minutes, no notes, true, from memory,
judged live. That format is a merciless laboratory. Every week Matthew Dicks watched people with
*better raw material than his* lose — and people with a trip-to-the-pharmacy story win. Over hundreds
of slams he reverse-engineered *why*. The winners weren't more dramatic; they were doing specific,
learnable things to manage the audience's attention second by second. *Storyworthy* names those
things.

The central realization behind all of them is a reframe of what suspense even is. Amateurs think
suspense comes from **big external danger** — will the bomb go off, will he survive the crash. But in
a true, small, personal story there usually *is* no bomb. Dicks' insight, earned on stage, is that
suspense in a personal story comes from something much smaller and more portable: **a question the
audience wants answered.** As long as the audience is holding an open question they care about, they
cannot leave — an open loop in the mind demands closing. The moment there are no open questions, or
the audience already knows how it ends, attention dies. So the entire craft of telling reduces to a
single job: **open questions in the audience's mind early, and don't answer them until the end.** The
devices — stakes, elephant, backpack, crystal ball — are all *mechanisms for opening and sustaining
those questions.* Once you see that, they stop being a random bag of tips and become one coherent
theory of attention.

A second, quieter idea runs under Dicks' craft: **honesty and smallness beat polish and drama.** The
Moth punishes anything that smells like performance or exaggeration; the audience can feel a lie.
Winners are relentlessly specific, vulnerable, and true. So the devices below are never about
inflating a story into something bigger than it was — they're about *revealing* the tension that was
genuinely there, in a small true moment, and letting the audience feel it.

## Why it exists (the pain it solves)

**Pain 1 — the flat telling.** You have a real five-second moment, but you narrate the events in a
calm, complete, chronological way and the tension quietly bleeds out. Nothing is *pulling* the
audience forward, because you've left no open questions. This is the single most common way a
perfectly good story dies in the telling.

**Pain 2 — starting by defusing your own bomb.** Most people open a story by *telling you how it ends*
("So this is the story of how I finally quit my job…"). That one sentence answers the main question
before the story starts, so there's nothing left to wonder about. The devices exist to do the
opposite: to *plant* questions, not resolve them.

**Pain 3 — no emotional texture.** Even a suspenseful story can feel thin if the audience doesn't
care about *this specific person*. Some devices (backpack, elephant) exist not to create suspense but
to make the audience *feel* — to attach them to the teller so the eventual change lands with weight.

## What it is

Four named devices, plus the sentence-level spine. Each is a tool for managing the audience's
attention and feeling.

### 1. Stakes — the engine of suspense

**Stakes** are the answer to the audience's unspoken question: *"Why should I care what happens
next?"* Crucially, Dicks means stakes in the *personal, human-sized* sense — not life-or-death, but
"something the teller wants, fears, or could lose." A story has stakes when the audience knows what's
at risk for you emotionally. No stakes = no reason to lean in. The other three devices are all
*specific techniques for injecting stakes* when a quiet true story doesn't wear them on its sleeve.

### 2. The Elephant — the promise you plant up front

**The Elephant** is a big, unmissable thing you place in the room *early* and then leave standing
there, unaddressed, so the audience keeps glancing at it. It's a planted question. You mention, near
the start, something looming — "the phone call I was about to get," "the last normal morning of my
life" — and then *move on to something ordinary*. The audience now carries an open loop: *wait, what
was the phone call?* They can't leave until you close it. The elephant is your primary tool for
opening a dominant question at the top and holding it to the end.

### 3. The Backpack — loading the audience up before a hard moment

**The Backpack** is a technique for maximizing suspense right *before* a key moment by making the
audience feel everything the character is carrying into it — their hopes, fears, and everything
that's at stake — so that when the moment arrives, the audience is as loaded up with anticipation as
the character. You "fill the backpack" by pausing on the threshold of the big moment and voicing what
the character wants and dreads *before* they walk through the door. Done right, the audience holds
their breath *with* you. The name is the image: you strap the same heavy pack onto the audience that
the character is wearing, so they feel its weight on the walk.

### 4. The Crystal Ball — a false prediction that spikes suspense

**The Crystal Ball** is when you have the character *predict* what's about to happen — and set up the
audience to expect that prediction to come true (or dread that it will) — creating suspense about
whether it does. You voice an expectation ("I was sure I'd bombed the interview," "I knew exactly how
this would go") which makes the audience lean toward that future, and then the story can confirm or
subvert it. It manufactures a question — *is the prediction right?* — where a flat telling would just
report the outcome.

### 5. The But / Therefore spine — the sentence-level engine

Underneath all of it is a rule for how *scenes connect*, and it's the same domino law you met in
Aristotle and Pixar, sharpened into a linguistic test. A well-built story connects its beats with
**"but"** and **"therefore"**, never **"and then."** "And then" is a bag of marbles (mere sequence);
"but" introduces conflict (a complication), "therefore" introduces consequence (causality). If you
can only say "and then" between two beats, they're not really a story yet.

**Mental model.** Think of the audience's attention as a **set of open browser tabs.** Every question
you plant opens a tab the audience can't close until you give them the answer — and an open tab
*itches*. Stakes decide whether a tab is worth opening at all (do they care?). The elephant opens one
big tab at the top and keeps it open the whole time. The backpack turns up that tab's brightness
right before a key click. The crystal ball opens a "will this prediction come true?" tab. The
but/therefore spine is the rule that every new tab must be *caused by* the last one, not just opened
at random. Your job as teller: keep at least one itchy tab open at all times, and close them all only
at the very end.

**Real-world analogy.** A magician doesn't start by showing you the coin is gone. They make you
*stare at the closed hand* — "there's a coin in here" (the elephant), "watch closely, this is where
it disappears" (the backpack, loading you up on the threshold), "you think it went up my sleeve"
(the crystal ball, planting a prediction) — and only at the end do they open the hand. The whole art
is *withholding the reveal while making you desperate for it.* Dicks' devices are that same
sleight-of-attention, applied to a true story.

## How it works (under the hood)

Take the true story seed from lesson 001 — *irritated at Dad's slow driving to the airport, then saw
his hands shaking and understood he's getting old* — and watch the devices transform a flat telling
into a gripping one.

**The flat telling (no devices, all "and then"):**

```
My dad drove me to the airport. He drove slowly and it annoyed me.
Then I noticed his hands were shaking on the wheel.
Then I realized he was getting old. Then I felt sad. Then I caught my flight.
```

Everything true, nothing withheld, all marbles. The audience has no open tab — you *told* them the
realization and the feeling, so there's nothing left to wonder. Dead on arrival.

**Now install the devices, one at a time:**

```
ELEPHANT (plant a question at the top, then walk away from it):
  "I didn't know it yet, but that car ride was the last time I'd think of my father
   as invincible."  → opens a tab: what happens in this car ride? — then you go ORDINARY.

STAKES (make ordinary irritation matter by naming what's underneath):
  You establish that you and Dad have always sparred; his competence is the thing
  you measure yourself against. Now his driving isn't just annoying — his strength
  is quietly the thing at risk. The audience knows what could be lost.

BACKPACK (load them up on the threshold of the key moment):
  Just before the shaking hands, slow down. Voice what you're carrying: the low-grade
  contempt, the certainty that he's indestructible, the whole lifetime of him being
  the strong one. The audience walks up to the windshield wearing that same pack.

CRYSTAL BALL (plant a prediction to steer expectation):
  "I figured he was driving slow to annoy me. He always did stuff like that on purpose."
  → the audience now expects a petty-dad beat. So the shaking hands SUBVERT the prediction
     and land twice as hard.

BUT / THEREFORE (connect the beats with conflict + consequence, never 'and then'):
  I was ready to snap at him — BUT I glanced at his hands. His grip was shaking —
  THEREFORE the joke died in my throat — THEREFORE I understood, in one second, that
  I was going to lose him.
```

Now trace what the audience *experiences*, tab by tab:

```
top      → ELEPHANT opens the big tab: "what makes this the last invincible-dad day?"
middle   → STAKES make them care; CRYSTAL BALL opens a second tab: "is he really just
           being petty?"; the telling stays ordinary so the elephant tab keeps itching
threshold→ BACKPACK cranks the brightness: they're holding their breath with you
moment   → the shaking hands: CRYSTAL BALL prediction shatters + ELEPHANT tab snaps shut
           at the same instant → this IS the five-second moment from lesson 001
end      → all tabs closed together → catharsis
```

Look at what happened. You didn't add drama or exaggerate — every added line is *true*. You *revealed
the tension that was already in the moment* by withholding the reveal and planting questions around
it. That's the whole craft: same true story, but told so the audience is never without an open,
itchy tab until the five-second moment closes them all at once.

And notice the devices aren't independent tricks — they're all serving the *one* job named in the
Origin Story: keep a question the audience cares about open, and don't answer it until the end. The
elephant opens the main question, stakes make it worth caring about, the backpack intensifies it, the
crystal ball adds a second one, and but/therefore guarantees each beat is *caused* by the last so the
chain never goes slack.

## How to implement it

This is a telling craft, so the "implementation" is a rehearse-and-check process, with a small typed
scaffold to force the devices to be present.

**Step 1 — model the telling, not just the story.** Extend the `TrueStory` type from lesson 001 with
the attention devices, so a bare five-second moment can't masquerade as a finished *telling*.

```typescript
interface StoryTelling {
  fiveSecondMoment: string;   // the internal change (from lesson 001)
  elephant: string;           // the question planted up front and left open
  stakes: string;             // what the teller wants / fears / could lose
  backpack: string;           // what to voice on the threshold of the key moment
  crystalBall?: string;       // an optional prediction that steers expectation
  beats: { text: string; connector: "but" | "therefore" | "and then" }[];
}
```

**Step 2 — a checker for the failures that kill a telling.** Again: heuristics, not proofs. But they
catch exactly the mistakes that make eyes drift.

```typescript
function critiqueTelling(t: StoryTelling): string[] {
  const notes: string[] = [];

  // The elephant must exist — without a planted question, there's no open tab.
  if (!t.elephant || t.elephant.length < 8) {
    notes.push("No elephant: plant a looming question up front and then walk away from it.");
  }

  // Stakes must be nameable, or the audience has no reason to lean in.
  if (!t.stakes || t.stakes.length < 8) {
    notes.push("No stakes: name what you want, fear, or could lose — or nobody cares what happens.");
  }

  // 'and then' is the tell of a marble. A few are survivable; a majority means no spine.
  const andThens = t.beats.filter(b => b.connector === "and then").length;
  if (t.beats.length > 0 && andThens / t.beats.length > 0.4) {
    notes.push("Marble chain: too many 'and then' beats — recut them as 'but' (conflict) or 'therefore' (consequence).");
  }

  // The backpack should land right before the moment, not be missing.
  if (!t.backpack || t.backpack.length < 8) {
    notes.push("Empty backpack: before the key moment, voice what you're carrying so we hold our breath with you.");
  }

  // Don't answer the elephant too early — the moment IS the payoff.
  if (t.beats[0]?.text.toLowerCase().includes(t.fiveSecondMoment.toLowerCase().slice(0, 12))) {
    notes.push("Defused bomb: you revealed the moment in the opening beat — withhold it until the end.");
  }

  return notes.length ? notes : ["Suspense engine is armed. Now rehearse it out loud and cut every word that isn't true."];
}
```

**Step 3 — run it on a broken telling** to feel the checks bite:

```typescript
const flat: StoryTelling = {
  fiveSecondMoment: "I realized my father was getting old and I'd lose him.",
  elephant: "",                       // nothing planted
  stakes: "",                         // no stated stakes
  backpack: "",                       // no threshold loading
  beats: [
    { text: "Dad drove me to the airport.", connector: "and then" },
    { text: "He drove slowly and it annoyed me.", connector: "and then" },
    { text: "His hands were shaking.", connector: "and then" },
    { text: "I felt sad.", connector: "and then" },
  ],
};

console.log(critiqueTelling(flat));
// [
//   "No elephant: plant a looming question up front and then walk away from it.",
//   "No stakes: name what you want, fear, or could lose — or nobody cares what happens.",
//   "Marble chain: too many 'and then' beats — recut them as 'but' or 'therefore'.",
//   "Empty backpack: before the key moment, voice what you're carrying so we hold our breath with you."
// ]
```

**Step 4 — the human step the linter can't do: rehearse aloud and cut to the true bones.** A checker
can confirm the devices are *present*; only your ear can confirm they're *honest and tight*. Dicks'
own practice is to tell a story many times, live, watching where the room's attention dips, and
surgically cutting anything that isn't serving an open question. Two rehearsal rules worth stealing:
say it out loud (silent editing lies to you about rhythm), and after each pass ask "what's the one
question keeping them here right now?" — if you can't answer it for any 20-second stretch, that
stretch is where they'll reach for their phone.

## Trade-offs, when to use, and pitfalls

**When to reach for these.** Any *told* personal story where you need to hold attention in real time:
interviews, toasts, talks, pitches, the dinner table. They're the layer on top of lesson 001 — first
find the five-second moment, *then* apply these to make the telling grip.

**When they're the wrong tool / can backfire.** In very short or casual tellings, a full elephant +
backpack + crystal ball is overkill and reads as manipulative theatre — pick one device, not four. In
written fiction, these have close cousins but the *live, spoken* framing (voicing predictions,
loading the backpack in the moment) is calibrated for a listening audience, not a reader. And none of
them fix a story that has no five-second moment — devices amplify a real change; they can't
manufacture one out of nothing.

**Common pitfalls:**

- **Defusing your own bomb in the first sentence.** "This is the story of how I finally forgave my
  dad" answers the main question before you start. Never open by revealing the ending — plant the
  elephant instead.
- **Confusing stakes with disaster.** Stakes are *personal and human-sized* — what you want, fear,
  or could lose. You don't need a life-or-death situation; you need the audience to know your heart
  is on the line. Over-inflating stakes into fake melodrama reads as a lie.
- **An elephant you never pay off.** If you plant a looming question and then forget it or resolve it
  limply, the audience feels cheated — the tab you opened never got the satisfying close. Every
  elephant must be *paid off*, ideally at the five-second moment.
- **"And then" everywhere.** The marble tell. If your beats only connect with "and then," you have a
  sequence, not a story. Force each junction to be a "but" (complication) or "therefore"
  (consequence).
- **Backpack in the wrong place.** Loading up the audience works right *before* the key moment. Do it
  too early and the tension dissipates before the payoff; skip it and the moment arrives without the
  held breath that makes it land.
- **Devices over honesty.** The Moth punishes performance. If applying a device tempts you to
  exaggerate or invent, stop — a smaller true version beats a bigger false one every time. The
  devices *reveal* real tension; they must never fabricate it.

## 🧠 Q&A Bank

**1. In a small, true, personal story with no external danger, where does suspense actually come
from?**
From open questions the audience cares about. Suspense isn't about bombs or crashes — it's the itch
of an unanswered question the audience is holding. As long as at least one such question is open,
attention stays; the instant there are none (or the ending is already known), attention dies. Every
device is a way to open and sustain those questions.

**2. What is the Elephant, and what job does it do?**
A big, looming thing you plant near the start and then leave unaddressed while you go on to something
ordinary. It opens a dominant question ("wait — what about *that*?") the audience carries the whole
time and can't put down until you pay it off, ideally at the five-second moment. It's your primary
tool for holding a question from the top to the end.

**3. What is the Backpack, and when do you use it?**
A technique for maximizing suspense *right before* a key moment: you pause on the threshold and voice
everything the character is carrying — their hopes, fears, what's at stake — so the audience is
loaded up with the same anticipation and holds their breath with you. Timing is the whole trick: it
belongs immediately before the pivotal beat, not earlier.

**4. What is the Crystal Ball, and why does a *false* prediction increase suspense?**
It's having the character voice a prediction about what's coming, steering the audience to expect
that outcome. It creates a new question — "will the prediction come true?" — where a flat telling
would just report the result. A prediction the story then subverts hits especially hard, because the
audience leaned toward one future and got another.

**5. What's the difference between "and then," "but," and "therefore," and why does it matter?**
"And then" is mere sequence — a bag of marbles with no causal link (a non-story). "But" introduces
conflict/complication; "therefore" introduces consequence/causality. Connecting beats with but/
therefore forces each event to arise from the last (Aristotle's dominoes at the sentence level),
which is what makes a telling feel like a story instead of a list.

**6. "What happens if…" — a teller opens with "So this is the story of how I finally quit my job."
Why does that hurt the story?**
Because it answers the main question before the story begins — it defuses the bomb. There's no
elephant left to wonder about, so no open tab, so no pull forward. The fix is to withhold the outcome
and plant a looming question instead, paying it off only at the moment of change.

**7. Dicks insists stakes don't need to be life-or-death. Why, and what's the risk of ignoring
that?**
Because personal-story stakes are human-sized — what the teller wants, fears, or could lose — and
audiences connect to that emotional risk, not to spectacle. Ignoring it tempts you to inflate the
story into fake melodrama, which a listening audience reads as a lie, killing the trust the whole
telling depends on.

**8. How do these four devices relate to each other — are they separate tricks?**
No — they're all mechanisms serving one job: keep a question the audience cares about open until the
end. Stakes make a question worth caring about; the elephant opens the main one and holds it; the
backpack intensifies it on the threshold; the crystal ball opens a second one; and but/therefore
keeps every beat caused by the last so the chain never goes slack. One theory of attention, four
tools.

**9. How does this lesson connect back to Aristotle and the five-second moment?**
The elephant/backpack/crystal-ball all build toward, and pay off at, the five-second moment of change
(lesson 001) — which is Aristotle's peripeteia + anagnorisis in a true story. But/therefore is his
unity of action at the sentence level. These devices are the *delivery system* that makes the
audience feel the single internal turn the whole story exists for.

## 🛠️ Hands-on Exercise

Take the story you mined in lesson 001's exercise (the one with a real belief-before/belief-after
flip). Now build the *telling*:

1. Write one **elephant** sentence you could open with that plants a looming question without
   revealing the outcome.
2. Write your **stakes** in one line: what you wanted, feared, or could lose.
3. Write the **backpack** — the 2–3 sentences you'd say on the threshold, right before the
   five-second moment, to load the audience with what you were carrying.
4. Optionally add a **crystal ball**: a prediction your character voices that the moment then
   confirms or subverts.
5. Rewrite the whole thing so no two beats connect with "and then" — only "but" or "therefore."

Then tell it out loud, timed, to one real person. Watch their face for the exact second attention
dips — that's a spot with no open question. Hint: if the room goes flat in the middle, your elephant
probably got answered too early, or you never planted one; re-check that the biggest question is
still open right up until the moment.

## ✅ Recap

- Suspense in a true story comes from **open questions the audience cares about** — keep at least one
  itchy "tab" open at all times, and close them only at the end.
- **Elephant:** plant a looming question up front, walk away from it, pay it off at the five-second
  moment. **Never open by revealing the ending.**
- **Stakes:** name what you want, fear, or could lose — human-sized, not life-or-death. No stakes, no
  reason to lean in.
- **Backpack:** right before the key moment, voice what you're carrying so the audience holds its
  breath with you. **Crystal Ball:** plant a prediction to steer (and then confirm or subvert)
  expectation.
- **But / Therefore, never "and then":** connect beats with conflict and consequence — Aristotle's
  dominoes at the sentence level. And through all of it: honesty and smallness beat polish and drama.
