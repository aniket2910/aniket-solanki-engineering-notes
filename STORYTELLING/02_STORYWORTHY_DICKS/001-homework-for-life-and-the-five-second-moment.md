# Finding Your Stories — Homework for Life & the Five-Second Moment

## The Core Question

Almost everyone believes the same thing about themselves: *"I don't have any good stories. Nothing
interesting has happened to me."* Meanwhile, someone at the same dinner table turns their trip to buy
milk into three minutes that has everyone leaning in. The question this lesson answers is the one
underneath all personal storytelling: **where do good stories actually come from — and why are you
convinced you have none when you're generating them every day and throwing them away?**

## The Origin Story (history & the thinking behind it)

To understand *Storyworthy*, you have to understand **The Moth**. The Moth is a storytelling
organization, founded in New York in 1997, built around a brutal, clarifying format called the
**StorySLAM**: ordinary people put their names in a hat, get called to a stage, and tell a *true*
story from their own life, **from memory, with no notes, in about five minutes**, judged by teams in
the audience. No script, no props, no lying. The constraints strip storytelling down to its bones —
you can't hide behind production value, so the only thing that can save you is a true story, well
found and well shaped.

**Matthew Dicks** is a schoolteacher and novelist who became one of the most successful Moth
StorySLAM competitors alive — dozens of wins. But the origin of his *method* is more interesting than
his trophy count, because it came from a fear, not a talent. Early on, Dicks was terrified he'd run
out of stories. He'd tell a good one, win, and then panic: *that was my one good story, and now it's
gone.* He assumed, like everyone does, that stories are rare — a handful of dramatic life events (the
car crash, the proposal, the big trip) that you spend down like a dwindling bank account.

The insight that became the whole book was the moment he realized that assumption was **exactly
backwards.** Stories aren't rare, huge, and external. They are **tiny, constant, and internal** — and
he was drowning in them without noticing. To prove it to himself, he started a daily practice (which
he named **Homework for Life**): every single night, he'd sit down and ask, *"What is the most
story-worthy moment of my day?"* and write one or two sentences capturing it. His expectation was
that most days would be blank — nothing happened. Instead, the opposite occurred: **every day had
something.** Not a car chase — a small moment where something *shifted*, where he felt or understood
something he hadn't that morning. Within weeks he had dozens of story seeds; within a year, hundreds.
The scarcity was an illusion the whole time.

The deeper realization — the thing that reorganizes how you see your own life — is *why* the practice
works. It's not just a note-taking habit. The act of looking back at each day *hunting* for the
story-worthy moment slowly **retrains your attention** so that you start noticing these moments *as
they happen*, in real time. You stop sleepwalking through your life. Dicks describes it as the
closest thing he's found to slowing down time — because a life you're paying attention to feels
fuller and longer than one you let blur past. So Homework for Life is two tools in one: a story mine
*and* a way of living more awake. That fusion — a craft technique that's secretly a life practice —
is the heart of *Storyworthy* and why the book resonates beyond the storytelling niche.

## Why it exists (the pain it solves)

**Pain 1 — the scarcity illusion.** People think story material is rare and dramatic, so they
conclude they don't have any (their life lacks explosions) and they stop looking. The whole
storytelling project dies at the source: not in the *telling*, but in the belief that there's nothing
to tell. Homework for Life attacks the problem at that root — it *proves to you*, with a growing list
you can't argue with, that your life is dense with material.

**Pain 2 — chasing the wrong kind of moment.** Even people who look for stories look for the wrong
thing: big events. But a big event ("I went skydiving!") is usually a bad story, because it's about
*spectacle*, not *change*. Dicks' framework redirects you to the moment that actually makes a story:
a small, internal shift. Without that redirection, you tell the loud version of your life and wonder
why no one leans in.

## What it is

Two linked ideas.

### 1. Homework for Life (the finding practice)

Every night, in a single spreadsheet or notebook, write the **date** and **one or two sentences**
capturing the most story-worthy moment of that day. Rules that make it work:

- It's *daily*. The habit, not any single entry, is the engine.
- It's *tiny*. One or two sentences. You're planting a seed you can regrow later, not writing the
  story now.
- It's *honest*. The story-worthy moment is often small, ordinary, even embarrassing — not the
  impressive thing you'd brag about.
- You do *not* need to know why a moment matters to record it. If it tugged at you, log it. The
  meaning often reveals itself months later.

### 2. Every story is about a five-second moment

This is the load-bearing craft claim of the whole book, and it's the thing to tattoo on your brain:

**A story is not about the events. A story is about a single moment — often about five seconds long —
where something *changed* inside the storyteller. Everything else in the story exists only to make
the audience feel that five-second change.**

The change is almost always internal and small: a realization, a decision, a shift in how you see
someone, the instant a feeling flipped. "I forgave my father." "I realized I was the bully." "I
understood, too late, that she'd been protecting me." The events — the plot — are just the *runway*
that lets that five-second moment take off. If a story has no such moment, it's an anecdote, not a
story. If you can name the five-second moment, you know what your story is *about* and what to cut
(everything that doesn't build toward it).

**Mental model.** Your life is a **riverbed full of gravel**, and story-worthy moments are the tiny
flecks of gold scattered through it. You've been standing on a fortune, convinced you're broke,
because you never looked down and *panned*. Homework for Life is the pan: run each day's gravel
through it and a fleck shows up nearly every time. The five-second-moment idea is the assayer's test
that tells you *which* fleck is actually gold — the ones where something *changed* — versus fool's
gold, the merely dramatic or impressive.

**Real-world analogy.** Think of a photographer who insists they have "no good photos" because they
never went anywhere exotic. Then they start carrying a camera every single day and shooting one frame
of ordinary life — the light on a stranger's face on the train, their kid's hand on a railing. A year
later they have a body of work more moving than any postcard from a famous landmark, because the
material was never *out there*; it was in the *attention*. Homework for Life is carrying that camera
for your inner life.

## How it works (under the hood)

Walk the full pipeline from a blank day to a told story.

```
STEP 1  Live the day (asleep at the wheel, like everyone).
STEP 2  That night, ask: "What was the most story-worthy moment today?"
        → forces a review; almost always finds a small shift.
STEP 3  Write 1–2 sentences. Date it. Store it. (Homework for Life)
STEP 4  Over weeks, the habit retrains STEP 1 — you start catching
        moments live, because your brain now knows it'll be asked at night.
STEP 5  Later, mining a seed for an actual telling:
          a) Find the FIVE-SECOND MOMENT — the internal change. Name it in one sentence.
          b) Ask: what did I believe/feel BEFORE that moment vs AFTER?
             (the change is the whole story; the gap is the point.)
          c) Build the minimal runway that makes the audience feel that gap.
             Cut everything that doesn't lead to the five-second moment.
```

Look closely at **Step 5a/5b** — this is where most people's "true stories" fail and where Dicks'
system fixes them. Untrained tellers structure a story around *what happened* (I did this, then this,
then this — a bag of marbles). Dicks structures it around *what changed in me* (I believed X; then
in one moment I believed the opposite). The events are demoted to *evidence for the change.* This is
the same move Aristotle and Pixar make (peripeteia / the want-need turn), but pointed at your real,
un-plotted life: the "reversal" in a true story is usually an **internal** reversal — a belief or
feeling flipping — not an external one.

A concrete example of the machinery. Suppose your Homework-for-Life entry reads: *"Got annoyed at Dad
for driving so slowly to the airport. Then noticed his hands were shaking on the wheel."*

- **The five-second moment** is not the drive, and not the airport. It's the *instant* your
  irritation turned into fear — the second you saw the shaking hands and understood, without words,
  *my father is getting old.*
- **Before:** he's the invincible dad who's always annoyed me. **After:** he's mortal, and I'm going
  to lose him. That gap is the entire story.
- **The runway:** you'd open in-scene in the car, build the ordinary irritation (so the audience
  lives your "before"), and then land the shaking hands so the audience feels the flip *with* you.
  Everything about the trip's itinerary, the flight, the traffic — cut, unless it serves that flip.

Notice what this does to the "I have no stories" problem. That car ride is *nothing* — the definition
of an unremarkable day. And yet it contains a complete, devastating story, because a five-second
internal change lived inside it. That's the proof of the book's thesis: story-worthiness is about
*change*, and change is everywhere, so stories are everywhere.

One more mechanism worth naming: **you often don't know a moment's meaning when it happens.** The
shaking-hands realization might land as just a flicker of unease in the moment; its full weight
arrives months later. This is *why* Homework for Life logs moments you can't yet explain — you're
capturing the fleck of gold before you know its value, so it's there when you're ready to assay it.
Memory won't hold these; they evaporate by morning. The written record is what makes the practice
compound.

## How to implement it

This is a life practice, not code — so here's the concrete worked setup and a first run.

**Step 1 — set up the ledger.** One spreadsheet. Three columns. That's the entire infrastructure.

```
| Date       | The moment (1–2 sentences)                                  | (later) The change / five-second moment |
|------------|-------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------|
| 2026-08-09 | Annoyed at Dad's slow driving. Then saw his hands shaking.   | irritation → fear: "he's mortal now"    |
| 2026-08-10 | A junior thanked me for a code review I almost skipped.     | (unclear yet — log it anyway)           |
```

The third column stays empty most nights. You fill it only when a moment's meaning surfaces — often
long after. Do not force it.

**Step 2 — the nightly ritual (60 seconds).** Before bed, ask the one question — *what was the most
story-worthy moment today?* — and write the sentence. If your honest answer is "genuinely nothing,"
write down the *most nothing* thing that still had a flicker in it. The flicker is the signal. Some of
the best stories start as "nothing" entries.

**Step 3 — after ~2 weeks, review.** Read your entries back. You'll notice two things: (a) you have
*way* more than you expected, and (b) your recent entries are richer than your early ones — because
the habit has already sharpened your live attention. That improvement curve is the practice working.

**Step 4 — mine one seed into a story.** Pick the entry that still tugs. Run Step 5 from the pipeline:

1. Write the **five-second moment** as a single sentence: "The moment I [internal change]."
2. Write your **before belief** and **after belief** in two lines. If they're not opposites, you
   haven't found the real moment — keep digging.
3. List the events, then **delete every event that doesn't build toward the flip.** What remains is
   your runway.

**A tiny structural helper.** If you like the typed-model habit from the Pixar lesson, here's the
personal-story shape reduced to what actually matters — notice how little of it is "events":

```typescript
interface TrueStory {
  fiveSecondMoment: string; // the single internal change — the WHOLE point
  beliefBefore: string;     // what you felt/thought before it
  beliefAfter: string;      // the opposite, after it  (must differ!)
  runway: string[];         // ONLY the events that make the audience feel the flip
}

function isReallyAStory(s: TrueStory): string {
  if (s.beliefBefore.trim().toLowerCase() === s.beliefAfter.trim().toLowerCase()) {
    return "Not a story yet — nothing changed. Find the moment where a belief/feeling flipped.";
  }
  if (s.runway.length === 0) {
    return "You have a moment but no runway — add just enough scene to make us feel the flip.";
  }
  return `Story is about: "${s.fiveSecondMoment}". Cut anything in the runway that doesn't lead here.`;
}
```

The check that matters is the first one: **if `beliefBefore` equals `beliefAfter`, you don't have a
story, you have an anecdote.** That single test, applied honestly, is most of what Dicks teaches about
*finding* the story inside your material.

## Trade-offs, when to use, and pitfalls

**When to reach for this.** Any time you tell a true story about yourself: interviews ("tell me about
a time you failed"), toasts, talks, essays, sales, or just being the person at dinner people actually
listen to. And as a standing life practice, for its own sake — the attention it builds is worth more
than the stories.

**When it's the wrong tool.** Homework for Life is for *true personal* storytelling. It won't plot a
novel or design a product narrative from scratch (that's Pixar/McKee territory). And the five-second
-moment lens can feel reductive for large, plotty fiction where multiple changes interweave — it's
sharpest on the compact, single-change true story the Moth format rewards.

**Common pitfalls:**

- **Waiting for a "big" day.** The impulse to only log dramatic events is the exact scarcity illusion
  the practice is meant to cure. Log the small internal moments — those *are* the gold.
- **Skipping the log because "I'll remember."** You won't. These moments evaporate overnight, and the
  ones whose meaning hasn't surfaced yet are precisely the ones you'll lose. The written ledger is
  non-negotiable.
- **Telling the events instead of the change.** The classic failure: a well-narrated sequence of
  what-happened with no internal flip. Technically accurate, emotionally flat. Always name the
  five-second moment first, then build backward.
- **Forcing meaning too early.** Some entries won't reveal their point for months. Don't discard a
  moment because you can't yet explain why it mattered — that flicker is the gold showing before
  you've assayed it.
- **Making it about being impressive.** Personal stories connect through *vulnerability and change*,
  not through how competent or heroic you looked. The moment you realized you were *wrong* is usually
  a better story than the moment you were right.

## 🧠 Q&A Bank

**1. Why do most people wrongly believe they have "no good stories"?**
Because they assume stories are rare, large, external events (crashes, proposals, big trips), so a
normal life looks empty of them. Dicks' insight is that stories are actually tiny, constant, and
*internal* — small moments of change happening every day — so the material was never missing, only
unnoticed and unrecorded.

**2. What is Homework for Life, and what problem does it solve?**
A daily practice: each night, write one or two sentences capturing the most story-worthy moment of
your day. It solves the scarcity illusion by producing a growing, undeniable record that your
ordinary life is dense with story material — and it retrains your attention to notice such moments
live.

**3. What does Dicks mean by the "five-second moment," and why is it the center of a story?**
It's the single, roughly five-second instant where something *changed inside* the storyteller — a
realization, a decision, a flip in feeling. It's the center because a story *is* that change; every
other part exists only to make the audience feel it. No such moment, no story — just an anecdote.

**4. "What happens if…" — a true story has no internal change, just a well-told sequence of events.
What is it, and why does it fall flat?**
It's an anecdote, not a story. It falls flat because there's no *turn* for the audience to feel — no
before-and-after gap in the teller. Events without a change are a bag of marbles; the emotional
payload of a personal story lives entirely in the shift, which this one lacks.

**5. Homework for Life is described as "slowing down time." What does that mean, and why does it
happen?**
Reviewing each day to find its story-worthy moment forces you to pay real attention to your life
rather than letting it blur past, and over time you begin catching moments as they happen. A life
you're actively attending to registers as fuller and longer, so the practice makes time *feel*
slower — a life benefit separate from the stories it produces.

**6. Why should you log moments even when you don't yet understand why they matter?**
Because a moment's meaning often surfaces months later, and if you didn't capture it, it's gone —
memory won't hold it overnight. You're recording the fleck of gold before you can assay its value, so
it's available when its significance finally becomes clear. Forcing meaning in the moment isn't
required.

**7. Why is a small, embarrassing moment often a better story than an impressive, dramatic one?**
Because personal stories connect through vulnerability and change, not spectacle or competence. A big
dramatic event is usually about what *happened* (external, impressive); a small embarrassing moment
is usually where a real internal shift lived. The moment you were wrong, or scared, or forgiven tends
to carry more emotional truth than the moment you were heroic.

**8. How does the "five-second moment" map onto Aristotle and Pixar?**
It's the same turn, aimed at real life. Aristotle's peripeteia + anagnorisis (reversal + recognition)
and Pixar's want/need climax are *external* turns in fiction; the five-second moment is the
*internal* version in a true story — a belief or feeling flipping. In all three, the story is built
so the audience feels that single turn, and everything else is runway.

## 🛠️ Hands-on Exercise

Start Homework for Life *tonight*, and do it for at least five consecutive days before judging it.
One spreadsheet, three columns (date / the moment / the change). Each night, write one or two
sentences answering "what was the most story-worthy moment today?" — and force yourself to log
something even on the "nothing happened" days.

On day 5, pick your single most tugging entry and mine it: name the five-second moment in one
sentence, write your belief-before and belief-after as two opposing lines, then list the events and
strike out every one that doesn't build toward the flip. Hint: if your before and after aren't
genuine opposites, you've named the wrong moment — the real one is usually a little earlier or a
little more embarrassing than the one you first reach for.

## ✅ Recap

- You are not out of stories — you're throwing away several a day. The belief in scarcity is the only
  real obstacle, and it's false.
- **Homework for Life:** each night, write one or two sentences on the most story-worthy moment of
  your day. The habit both mines stories and retrains your live attention (it "slows down time").
- **Every story is about a five-second moment** — a small *internal change*. The story *is* that
  change; all events are just runway to make the audience feel it.
- The decisive test of a true story: **belief-before must differ from belief-after.** If nothing
  flipped, it's an anecdote, not a story.
- Small and embarrassing beats big and impressive, because connection comes from change and
  vulnerability, not spectacle.
