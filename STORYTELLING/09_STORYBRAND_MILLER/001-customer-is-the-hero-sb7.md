# The Customer Is the Hero — the SB7 Framework

## The Core Question

Look at almost any company's website or any nervous founder's pitch and you'll see the same instinct:
"We are amazing. We were founded in 2011. We are passionate about innovation. We have won awards. Look
how great *we* are." And it doesn't work — visitors bounce, investors glaze over. Meanwhile a
different message, saying almost nothing about the company, converts. **Why does talking about how
great you are repel the very people you're trying to win — and what should you say instead?** Donald
Miller's answer is a single positioning flip that, once you see it, you can't unsee.

## The Origin Story (history & the thinking behind it)

**Donald Miller** is a bestselling author and the founder of a marketing company. His path to
StoryBrand is itself the insight: he'd spent years as a *storyteller* (a memoirist), and when he moved
into business, he realized companies were failing at marketing for a reason storytellers understood
and marketers didn't — **they were breaking the basic rules of story.** Specifically, they were casting
*themselves* as the hero of the narrative. And Miller knew from story craft that the audience doesn't
come to a story to admire the mentor; they come to *identify with the hero and go on the hero's
journey.* A company shouting "we're the hero" is asking the customer to watch *someone else's* story —
and nobody wants that. In **2017** he distilled this into **_Building a StoryBrand: Clarify Your
Message So Customers Will Listen_** and its **SB7 framework** (StoryBrand 7).

The intellectual lineage is explicit and direct: SB7 is **Campbell's Hero's Journey (Chapter 03)
translated into marketing**, with one crucial role-swap. In Campbell, a hero with a problem meets a
mentor (a Gandalf, an Obi-Wan, a Yoda) who gives them wisdom and a tool, and then the *hero* goes and
wins. Miller's core move: **your customer is Luke Skywalker; your brand is Yoda.** The customer is the
hero on a journey; your company is the *guide* who has already walked this road, understands the
hero's struggle, and hands them the plan and the tool to win their *own* victory. Every failing
"we're the hero" brand is really a Yoda who wandered onstage demanding to be treated like Luke.

There's a second idea powering the framework, drawn straight from cognitive reality (and echoing the
Heaths' Chapter 08): **the brain is wired to conserve energy, and confusion burns energy, so a
confusing message gets ignored.** Miller's relentless refrain is *"if you confuse, you lose."* Human
attention flees anything that requires effort to parse. So SB7 isn't just about story roles — it's
about *radical clarity*: cutting the noise until the customer instantly understands what you offer, how
it improves their life, and what to do next. The story structure is the tool for achieving that
clarity, because story is the format the brain processes most effortlessly.

## Why it exists (the pain it solves)

Two pains, tightly linked.

**Pain 1 — the self-absorbed brand.** Companies talk about themselves (their history, their passion,
their features) because it feels natural and safe. But it casts the customer as a spectator to the
company's story, which generates no desire and no action. SB7 solves this by forcing the flip: reframe
every message so the *customer* is the hero pursuing *their* goal, and the brand is merely the guide
who helps. Suddenly the customer sees *themselves* winning — which is what actually motivates a
purchase.

**Pain 2 — confusion.** Even well-meaning brands drown the customer in information — every feature,
every differentiator, jargon, cleverness — so the customer can't quickly grasp the one thing that
matters to them. Confusion costs the sale ("if you confuse, you lose"). SB7 solves this by imposing a
clear story spine that answers the only three questions a customer's brain is really asking: *What do
you offer? How will it make my life better? What do I do to get it?*

## What it is

**Precise definition.** SB7 is a 7-part message template based on story structure, in which the
customer is cast as the hero and the brand as the guide, arranged so a prospect instantly understands
the value offered and the action to take. The seven parts:

```
1. A CHARACTER          The customer is the hero. Start with what THEY want (one clear desire).
                        (Not you. Them.)
2. HAS A PROBLEM        The hero has a problem — on three levels:
                          • EXTERNAL: the tangible obstacle (e.g. "my software keeps crashing").
                          • INTERNAL: how it makes them FEEL (e.g. "I feel incompetent and stressed").
                          • PHILOSOPHICAL: why it's just plain WRONG (e.g. "software shouldn't fight you").
                        Brands sell to the external problem; customers buy to solve the INTERNAL one.
3. AND MEETS A GUIDE    The brand enters — NOT as hero but as guide — showing two things:
                          • EMPATHY: "we understand your struggle."
                          • AUTHORITY: "we've solved this before" (proof, experience, credibility).
4. WHO GIVES A PLAN     The guide lays out a simple, clear plan (usually 3 steps) that removes the
                        fear of doing business — the hero can now see the path.
5. AND CALLS TO ACTION  A clear, direct call to action ("Buy Now", "Schedule a Call"). Heroes don't
                        act unless challenged to. Ambiguity = no action.
6. THAT HELPS AVOID     Name the stakes of failure — what's at risk if they DON'T act. (There's no
   FAILURE               story without stakes; the customer must feel what's at stake.)
7. AND ENDS IN SUCCESS  Paint the vivid, specific picture of how good life looks AFTER the hero wins
                        with your help. Show them the transformed self.
```

The engine underneath is what Miller calls **identity transformation**: a great brand story shows the
hero moving from one identity to a better one ("from overwhelmed amateur → confident professional").
People don't buy products; they buy a *better version of themselves*, and your guide is who gets them
there.

**Mental model.** SB7 is **a movie trailer where the customer is the star and you're the director who
stays off-screen.** A trailer works by making the *viewer* imagine themselves in the hero's shoes —
facing the problem, meeting the guide, getting the plan, and ending triumphant. The director's genius
is invisible; you feel it only as the hero's clear, exciting journey. The instant the director walks
into frame yelling "look how talented I am," the spell breaks. Your brand is the director: your success
is the *customer's* clarity, not your own applause.

**Real-world analogy.** Picture a **personal trainer** done right. A bad trainer talks endlessly about
their own certifications, their gym's history, their philosophy of fitness (the self-absorbed brand).
A great trainer says: "*You* want to feel strong and confident (hero + desire). Right now your back
hurts and you feel embarrassed at the gym (external + internal problem). I get it — I've helped
hundreds of people exactly like you (empathy + authority). Here's your simple 3-step plan (plan). Book
your first session (call to action). Keep going the way you are and the pain gets worse (failure). Six
months from now you'll move without thinking about it and feel like yourself again (success)." The
trainer barely mentions themselves — and that's *why* you sign up.

## How it works (under the hood)

The mechanism is the **hero/guide role-swap plus the three levels of problem**, aimed at radical
clarity. Two things do the heavy lifting.

**1. Guide, not hero — and why it's counterintuitively more powerful.**

```
WRONG (brand as hero):     "We are the best. We are innovators. We win awards." 
                           → customer is a spectator; no desire, no action.
RIGHT (brand as guide):    "YOU want X. We understand, and we've helped others get X. Here's how."
                           → customer is the hero; they see THEMSELVES winning → desire → action.
```

It feels backwards — surely you should promote your greatness? But story logic is clear: the audience
identifies with the hero, and there's only room for one hero. By *lowering* yourself to guide, you
*raise* the customer to hero, and it's the hero's desire that drives the story forward toward the
purchase. Yoda has more influence over the story than any character *precisely because* he's not
trying to be Luke. The guide's power is quiet and total.

**2. The three levels of problem — and the one customers actually pay to solve.**

This is Miller's most useful subtlety. Every problem operates on three levels, and brands habitually
address only the shallowest:

```
EXTERNAL       the surface obstacle           "my accounting is a mess"       ← brands market to this
INTERNAL       how it makes them FEEL          "I feel anxious and behind"     ← customers BUY to fix this
PHILOSOPHICAL  why it's wrong on principle     "I shouldn't have to fear tax season"  ← makes it a cause
```

The insight: **people buy to resolve the *internal* problem.** Nobody wants accounting software; they
want to *stop feeling anxious about their finances.* A brand that speaks only to the external problem
("automated ledger reconciliation") competes on features; a brand that names the *internal* feeling
("stop dreading tax season — feel in control of your money") connects and converts. And naming the
*philosophical* level ("you deserve to feel confident about your business") turns a transaction into a
cause worth caring about. This is Chapter 08's "emotional" and Chapter 07's "internal struggle,"
operationalized for marketing: sell to the feeling, not the feature.

**3. Stakes and transformation — the story frame that creates urgency.**

Parts 6 (failure) and 7 (success) are the *stakes* — and you met in Cron and McKee that there's no
story, and no felt meaning, without stakes. By showing both what the hero *loses* if they don't act
(failure) and the vivid better life if they do (success), SB7 makes the customer *feel* the gap between
their current and transformed self — and that felt gap is desire. The whole framework is engineered so
the customer pictures a specific, better identity on the far side of doing business with you, and feels
the cost of staying where they are.

**The "grunt test" — the clarity check.** Miller's practical clarity test: could a caveman ("a grunt")
glancing at your website for 5 seconds answer three questions — (1) What do you offer? (2) How will it
make my life better? (3) What do I do to buy it? If not, you've failed the "if you confuse, you lose"
rule. The grunt test is the enforcement mechanism for the radical clarity the whole framework demands.

**A worked contrast:**

```
BRAND-AS-HERO (fails the grunt test, all about "us"):
  "Founded in 2014, Acme is a passionate team of innovators leveraging cutting-edge
   AI to revolutionize the future of workflow synergy."
   → What do they even do? How does it help ME? What do I do next? Unclear on all three.

SB7 (customer as hero, passes the grunt test):
  HERO + DESIRE:   "You want to spend your evenings with your family, not on paperwork."
  PROBLEM (int.):  "Right now invoicing eats your nights and you feel like you're drowning."
  GUIDE:           "We've helped 4,000 small businesses get their time back." (empathy + authority)
  PLAN:            "1. Connect your account. 2. We automate invoicing. 3. Get your evenings back."
  CALL TO ACTION:  "Start your free trial."
  FAILURE:         "Or keep losing evenings to admin, month after month."
  SUCCESS:         "Imagine closing the laptop at 5 and actually being present at dinner."
   → grunt test passed in 5 seconds: what, how-it-helps-me, and what-to-do. And I'm the hero.
```

## How to implement it

Model the SB7 spine and enforce the two things brands get wrong: the hero/guide swap and the internal
problem.

```typescript
interface StoryBrand {
  hero: string;              // MUST be the customer, not the company
  desire: string;           // what the customer (hero) wants
  problem: {
    external: string;       // the tangible obstacle
    internal: string;       // how it makes them FEEL  ← the one they buy to solve
    philosophical: string;  // why it's just wrong
  };
  guide: { empathy: string; authority: string }; // the BRAND, positioned as guide
  plan: string[];           // simple steps (ideally 3)
  callToAction: string;     // direct and clear
  failure: string;          // stakes of NOT acting
  success: string;          // vivid transformed life after winning
}

function critiqueStoryBrand(sb: StoryBrand): string[] {
  const notes: string[] = [];

  // The hero must be the customer. Heuristic: "we/our/us" as the hero is the classic failure.
  if (/\b(we|our|us|company)\b/i.test(sb.hero)) {
    notes.push("Brand-as-hero: the hero is the company. Recast the CUSTOMER as the hero.");
  }
  // Customers buy to solve the INTERNAL problem — it must be present.
  if (!sb.problem.internal) {
    notes.push("Feature-selling: no internal problem. Name how it makes them FEEL — that's what they buy.");
  }
  // A guide needs BOTH empathy and authority.
  if (!sb.guide.empathy || !sb.guide.authority) {
    notes.push("Weak guide: a guide needs both empathy ('we get it') and authority ('we've done it').");
  }
  // No CTA = no action. Heroes must be called to act.
  if (!sb.callToAction) {
    notes.push("No call to action: heroes don't act unless challenged. Add a direct CTA.");
  }
  // No stakes = no story. Failure must be named.
  if (!sb.failure) {
    notes.push("No stakes: name what's lost if they don't act, or there's no urgency.");
  }
  return notes.length ? notes : ["SB7 sound: customer is hero, internal problem named, guide credible, clear CTA and stakes."];
}
```

Run it on a typical "about us" blurb and the very first check fires: `Brand-as-hero`. That one flag is
80% of bad marketing. Fix it — recast the customer as hero, name their *internal* feeling, position
yourself as the empathetic-and-credible guide with a 3-step plan and a clear CTA — and the message
clarifies almost automatically. That's the implementation: not clever copy, but the *role-swap* plus
*radical clarity.*

## Trade-offs, when to use, and pitfalls

**When to reach for SB7.** Any outward-facing message meant to *move an audience to act*: websites,
landing pages, sales pitches, elevator pitches, fundraising decks, product one-pagers — and personal
branding (a résumé or LinkedIn where the *reader/employer* is subtly the hero you help succeed). It's
the most practically monetizable framework in the course and a fast fix for "our messaging is
confusing."

**When it can mislead or flatten.** SB7 is a *clarity and conversion* tool, deliberately simple and
formulaic — which is its strength for marketing and its weakness elsewhere. It's not a tool for
*fiction* (where casting the audience as hero doesn't apply), and applied too rigidly across a category,
it makes every brand's website sound identical (the same "you're the hero, we're the guide" cadence).
It also optimizes for *persuasion and action*, so like the Heaths' model it carries an ethical edge:
it works just as well for products that don't deserve the sale. Use it to clearly communicate genuine
value, not to manufacture false desire.

**Common pitfalls:**

- **Making yourself the hero.** The cardinal sin. Every "we're passionate innovators" sentence is a
  Yoda demanding to be Luke. Recast the customer as hero, always.
- **Selling to the external problem only.** Listing features that fix the tangible obstacle while
  ignoring the *internal* feeling the customer actually pays to resolve. Name the feeling.
- **Being a guide with only authority (or only empathy).** All credentials and no "we understand you"
  feels cold; all empathy and no proof feels incompetent. A guide needs both.
- **Confusion / failing the grunt test.** Cleverness, jargon, and information overload that a
  5-second glance can't parse. "If you confuse, you lose." Optimize for instant clarity over
  impressiveness.
- **No clear call to action or no stakes.** Heroes don't act unless challenged, and there's no urgency
  without something to lose. A vague "learn more" and no named failure kills conversion.

## 🧠 Q&A Bank

**1. What is the single core idea of StoryBrand?**
In your brand's story, the *customer* is the hero and your company is the *guide* (the mentor, like
Yoda to Luke), not the hero. Casting yourself as the hero makes the customer a spectator and kills
desire; positioning yourself as the guide who helps the customer win *their* journey is what drives
action.

**2. Why is positioning your brand as the guide more powerful than positioning it as the hero?**
Because audiences identify with the hero, and there's room for only one. By lowering yourself to guide,
you raise the customer to hero, and it's the hero's desire that drives the story toward the purchase.
Like Yoda, the guide has more real influence over the story precisely because they aren't competing to
be the hero.

**3. What are the three levels of a customer's problem, and which do they actually buy to solve?**
External (the tangible obstacle, e.g. messy accounting), internal (how it makes them feel, e.g.
anxious and behind), and philosophical (why it's just wrong, e.g. "I shouldn't have to dread tax
season"). Customers buy to solve the *internal* problem — the feeling — even though brands habitually
market only to the external one. Sell to the feeling, not the feature.

**4. What two things must a brand demonstrate to be a credible guide, and why both?**
Empathy ("we understand your struggle") and authority ("we've solved this before" — proof, experience,
results). Both are needed because authority without empathy feels cold and self-serving, while empathy
without authority feels sympathetic but incompetent. A trustworthy guide understands *and* is proven.

**5. What is the "grunt test," and what rule does it enforce?**
A clarity check: could someone glancing at your message for ~5 seconds answer (1) what you offer, (2)
how it makes their life better, and (3) what to do to buy it? It enforces Miller's rule "if you
confuse, you lose" — the brain flees anything requiring effort to parse, so radical clarity beats
cleverness and completeness.

**6. Why do parts 6 (failure) and 7 (success) matter — isn't naming failure negative?**
They're the *stakes*, and (as Cron and McKee establish) there's no story or felt meaning without
stakes. Showing what the hero loses by not acting (failure) and the vivid better life if they do
(success) makes the customer feel the gap between their current and transformed self — and that felt
gap is desire and urgency. Without stakes, there's no reason to act now.

**7. "What happens if…" — a company's homepage is all about their history, passion, and awards, and it
doesn't convert. What's the StoryBrand diagnosis and fix?**
Diagnosis: brand-as-hero and likely a failed grunt test — the company made itself the hero, so the
visitor is a spectator with no desire, and probably can't tell in 5 seconds what's offered or what to
do. Fix: recast the *customer* as hero (lead with what *they* want), name their internal problem,
position the company as the empathetic-and-credible guide with a simple plan and a clear CTA, and show
the stakes.

**8. How is SB7 related to Campbell's Hero's Journey?**
It's the monomyth translated into marketing with one role-swap: the customer is the hero (Luke) on a
journey, and the brand is the guide/mentor (Yoda/Obi-Wan) who provides empathy, authority, and a plan
so the hero can win their *own* victory. The customer's problem is the call, the plan is the mentor's
gift, and "success" is the return with the elixir — a transformed self.

## 🛠️ Hands-on Exercise

Take something you need to "sell" — your product, your team's project, or even yourself for a role — and
write its SB7 in seven lines: (1) name the *customer/audience* as hero and their **desire**; (2) their
problem on all three levels (external / **internal** / philosophical); (3) you as guide showing
**empathy + authority**; (4) a 3-step **plan**; (5) a direct **call to action**; (6) the **failure**
stakes; (7) the vivid **success**/transformed identity. Then apply the grunt test: hand it to someone
for 5 seconds and ask them what you offer, how it helps them, and what to do next. Hint: if your "hero"
line contains "we," "our," or your company name, you've already failed — rewrite it so the first word
is about *them*.

## ✅ Recap

- The core flip: **the customer is the hero; your brand is the guide** (Yoda, not Luke). Casting
  yourself as hero makes the customer a spectator and kills desire.
- **SB7's seven parts**: a character (hero) with a desire → has a problem (external/internal/
  philosophical) → meets a guide (empathy + authority) → who gives a plan → calls them to action →
  that helps avoid failure → and ends in success.
- Customers buy to solve the **internal problem** (the feeling), not the external one (the feature).
  Sell the transformed *identity*, not the product.
- **Stakes** (failure + success) create desire and urgency; a clear **call to action** is required
  because heroes don't act unless challenged.
- **"If you confuse, you lose"** — enforce radical clarity with the **grunt test** (what, how-it-helps,
  what-to-do, in 5 seconds). SB7 is Campbell's monomyth operationalized for business.
