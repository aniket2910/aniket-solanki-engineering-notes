# Organic Story: The 7 Steps, Moral Argument & the Character Web

## The Core Question

Snyder's beat sheet works — and yet a beat-sheet-perfect film can feel like a machine: correct,
hollow, interchangeable. That's because a template is imposed *from outside*, indifferent to what your
particular story is actually about. The question this chapter answers is the one that fixes that
hollowness: **can a story be grown from the inside — from the specific human being at its center and
the specific truth it wants to argue — so that its structure is unique and inevitable rather than
stamped on?** Truby says yes, and it changes how you think about plot, character, and theme forever.

## The Origin Story (history & the thinking behind it)

**John Truby** is, like McKee, a teacher — he's run story classes and consulted on hundreds of
Hollywood scripts for decades. But his book is explicitly a *reaction*. By the mid-2000s, the
screenwriting world had been saturated by exactly the tools you just learned: the three-act structure,
the Hero's Journey, and above all Snyder's beat sheet. Truby watched these become **formulas** — and
watched the formulas produce mechanical, predictable, morally shallow movies. His **_The Anatomy of
Story_** (2007) is, at its core, an argument that *template thinking is killing storytelling.*

His counter-thesis draws on an older, deeper idea: the **organic** conception of art, which goes back
at least to the Romantic critics (Coleridge and others in the early 1800s), who distinguished
"mechanical form" (a shape imposed from outside, like pressing dough into a cookie-cutter) from
"organic form" (a shape that grows from within, like a plant unfolding from a seed). Truby applies
this to story: a great story's structure should be **unique to that story**, growing out of its own
premise the way a tree's shape grows out of its particular seed and soil — not cut to fit a
one-size-fits-all outline. He calls the seed the **designing principle** — the deep organizing logic
specific to *this* story.

Truby's second big move is to place **morality at the center of storytelling** — not morality as
preaching, but story as a **moral argument.** His claim: every real story is secretly making a case
about *how to live* — about the right and wrong way to act to reach a goal. The hero begins acting the
wrong way (their moral weakness), the story tests that way against an opponent who embodies a competing
value, and the hero either learns a better way (self-revelation) or is destroyed by failing to. Plot,
in this view, is *the vehicle for a moral argument*, and character change *is* the argument being
proven. This is Aristotle's "character revealed through action" pushed all the way to "the entire
story is an ethical proof."

The third pillar is the **character web.** Truby's insight here is genuinely reorienting: you should
*never* design a character in isolation. A character means nothing alone; they mean something only in
*contrast* to the others. So you design all your major characters *as a system*, each defined by how
they oppose and illuminate the hero — every opponent and ally is a different possible answer to the
story's central moral question. Define the web, and character, theme, and plot generate each other.

So Truby is deliberately the anti-Snyder: where Snyder gives you the same 15 slots for every story,
Truby gives you a *method for growing a different structure for every story* — one rooted in a specific
moral weakness and a specific web of opposing values.

## Why it exists (the pain it solves)

Two pains, both downstream of template thinking.

**Pain 1 — the hollow, generic story.** When you fit a story to an external template, its structure
has nothing to do with its *particular* meaning, so it feels interchangeable and mechanical. Truby's
organic method solves this by deriving structure *from* the story's own premise and moral weakness, so
no two structures are alike and each feels inevitable.

**Pain 2 — the meaningless story.** A story can be structurally sound and *about nothing* — no moral
weight, no argument about how to live, so nothing at stake beneath the plot. Truby's "story as moral
argument" solves this by insisting the plot exists to test a way of living, giving every beat an
ethical charge. And the **character web** solves the related pain of the *flat supporting cast* —
characters who exist only to service the plot — by making every character a living embodiment of a
competing value.

## What it is

Three linked systems: the **7 key steps** (the organic spine), the **character web**, and **story as
moral argument.** (Truby also famously lays out **22 steps**, a finer-grained expansion — but the 7
are the load-bearing skeleton, so master those.)

### The 7 Key Steps (the organic spine)

Truby argues these seven arise *naturally* from any true story — they're not imposed, they're the
skeleton the premise grows. Note that they are almost entirely **internal/moral**, not plot events:

```
1. WEAKNESS & NEED   — the hero begins with a deep flaw that's ruining their life.
                       • psychological need: what they must overcome WITHIN themselves.
                       • moral need: how they must change the way they TREAT OTHERS.
                       Crucially, the hero does NOT know their real need at the start.
2. DESIRE            — the hero's conscious, external goal (what they think they want).
                       This drives the visible plot. (McKee's want; the surface engine.)
3. OPPONENT          — the person who wants the SAME goal, or blocks it, and who attacks the
                       hero's greatest weakness. The best opponent competes for the same thing
                       AND embodies the opposite moral value. (McKee's antagonism, moralized.)
4. PLAN              — the hero's strategy to defeat the opponent and reach the desire.
5. BATTLE            — the final conflict; the climactic clash of the two ways of living.
6. SELF-REVELATION   — the hero, through the battle, sees the truth about themselves and their
                       moral failing. This is the point of the whole story — the recognition.
7. NEW EQUILIBRIUM   — life resettles at a higher (or, in tragedy, lower) level; the change is
                       permanent. (Aristotle's end; the Spine's "and ever since.")
```

The heart of it is the loop between **step 1 (weakness/need)** and **step 6 (self-revelation)**: the
story exists to move the hero from *not knowing* their true moral failing to *seeing* it. Everything
between is the pressure that forces that recognition. Notice that unlike Snyder, there are no page
numbers and no fixed beats — the *shape* these seven take is different for every story, because it
grows from the specific weakness in step 1.

### Story as Moral Argument

The hero starts pursuing their desire the *wrong* (immoral or self-defeating) way — hurting others,
acting on their weakness. The opponent embodies a competing value. Through the battle, the hero's way
of acting is tested to destruction, and at self-revelation the hero (and audience) learns the *right*
way to act. The plot is the argument; the self-revelation is its conclusion. Theme, in Truby, isn't a
message you bolt on — it's the *outcome of the dramatized argument.*

### The Character Web & Four-Corner Opposition

Design characters as a *system of contrasts*, never alone. Truby's practical tool is **four-corner
opposition**: place the hero at one corner and arrange the main opponent and two secondary opponents
(or allies) at the other corners, each embodying a *different* value or worldview, so the story
examines its central question from four distinct moral angles. Each character is a different answer to
"what's the right way to live?" The hero is defined by *how they differ* from each.

**Mental model.** A Truby story is a **plant grown from a seed**, not furniture assembled from a kit.
The seed is the premise + the hero's moral weakness; the designing principle is the plant's genetic
plan; the 7 steps are the parts every plant has (root, stem, leaves, flower) but which take a unique
shape in every specimen. Snyder hands you a plastic plant, identical on every desk; Truby teaches you
to grow a real one, unique to its seed and soil.

**Real-world analogy.** Think of the character web like a **panel of witnesses in a courtroom trial
about how to live.** The story is prosecuting a question ("is ruthless ambition worth it?"). The hero
takes the stand acting one way; each opponent and ally is another witness embodying a different answer
— the cynic, the innocent, the pragmatist. The "self-revelation" is the verdict the hero reaches after
all the testimony (the battle) is heard. You'd never design one witness in isolation; each exists to
contrast with the others and pressure the hero toward the verdict.

## How it works (under the hood)

The engine is the **moral loop**, and it runs *underneath* the visible desire-plot. Trace both layers
at once:

```
VISIBLE LAYER (the plot):        MORAL LAYER (the real story):
  DESIRE  → the external goal      WEAKNESS/NEED → hero's flawed way of acting (unknown to them)
  OPPONENT → blocks the goal        the opponent EMBODIES the competing value, and ATTACKS the weakness
  PLAN    → strategy to win         the hero pursues the goal the WRONG (immoral/self-defeating) way
  BATTLE  → climactic clash         the two WAYS OF LIVING collide and one is proven bankrupt
  (win/lose the goal)               SELF-REVELATION → hero SEES their moral failing (the verdict)
  NEW EQUILIBRIUM                    the hero now lives the RIGHT way (or, in tragedy, can't)
```

The load-bearing relationship — and Truby's single most useful idea — is the bond between the
**opponent** and the **hero's weakness.** A weak story has an opponent who merely blocks the goal (a
generic bad guy). A great story has an opponent *precisely designed to attack the hero's specific moral
weakness.* If the hero's weakness is that he can't trust anyone, the ideal opponent is someone who
exploits exactly that — forcing the flaw into the open where it must be confronted. The opponent isn't
an obstacle; they're a *mirror and a scalpel*, cutting toward the self-revelation. This is why "design
the hero and opponent together" is Truby's core instruction: the opponent is generated *from* the
hero's weakness, and the plot is generated from their collision.

Watch the machinery on a real example — **_The Godfather_ (Michael Corleone):**

```
1. Weakness/Need   → Michael believes he's separate from his family's crime, morally clean.
                     Moral need: to see that vengeance and power will corrupt and isolate him.
                     (He does NOT know this need — he thinks he's the good son.)
2. Desire          → protect his family (and, increasingly, seize and hold power).
3. Opponent        → rival families / Sollozzo — but the DEEPEST opponent is the pull of the
                     family "business" itself, which attacks his belief that he's separate from it.
4. Plan            → he steps in "just this once," then deeper, each step "to protect the family."
5. Battle          → the climactic consolidation of power (the baptism murders).
6. Self-Revelation → the tragic inversion: instead of a redemptive recognition, Michael becomes
                     the very thing he swore he wasn't — and (in the arc across the films) is left
                     utterly isolated. The moral argument is proven in the NEGATIVE.
7. New Equilibrium → Michael is now the Don, and morally lost. Permanent, tragic change.
```

Notice this is a *tragedy* — the self-revelation is failed or too late — and the 7 steps handle it
without modification, because they're organic (about *this* character's moral trajectory), not a
victory-shaped template. Snyder's beat sheet, tuned for the triumphant mainstream arc, strains here;
Truby's organic spine fits, because it grew from Michael's specific weakness. That's the payoff of
organic over mechanical form: the same 7 steps produce a redemption in one story and a damnation in
another, each uniquely shaped.

And see the **moral argument** completed: the plot ("protect the family by taking power") *tests* a way
of living ("power in the service of family is justified"), and the story's verdict — proven through the
battle and Michael's isolation — is that this way of living destroys the soul it claims to protect.
That verdict *is* the theme, and it was never bolted on; it emerged from the collision of characters in
the web.

## How to implement it

Model the organic spine and the opponent–weakness bond, since that bond is the thing beginners most
need forced.

```typescript
interface OrganicStory {
  weakness: string;         // the hero's deep flaw ruining their life
  need: {
    psychological: string;  // what they must overcome within
    moral: string;          // how they must change the way they treat others
  };
  heroKnowsNeed: boolean;   // should be FALSE at the start
  desire: string;           // the conscious external goal (drives the plot)
  opponent: {
    who: string;
    competesForSameGoal: boolean;
    attacksWeakness: string; // HOW the opponent targets the hero's specific weakness
  };
  selfRevelation: string;   // the truth the hero sees at the climax (mirrors 'weakness')
}

function critiqueOrganic(s: OrganicStory): string[] {
  const notes: string[] = [];

  // The hero must NOT know their need at the start, or there's no arc to travel.
  if (s.heroKnowsNeed) {
    notes.push("No arc: the hero already knows their need. Hide it — they must DISCOVER it via the story.");
  }
  // The opponent must attack the specific weakness, not just block the goal.
  if (!s.opponent.attacksWeakness) {
    notes.push("Generic villain: the opponent doesn't attack the hero's weakness. Design them FROM the weakness.");
  }
  // Best opponents compete for the same goal (raises stakes and mirrors the hero).
  if (!s.opponent.competesForSameGoal) {
    notes.push("Consider: give the opponent the SAME goal — competition mirrors and pressures the hero.");
  }
  // Self-revelation must resolve the weakness — it's the point of the whole story.
  if (!s.selfRevelation) {
    notes.push("No point: there's no self-revelation. The story exists to move weakness → seeing the truth.");
  }
  return notes.length ? notes : ["Organic spine intact: hidden need, weakness-targeting opponent, real self-revelation."];
}
```

Run it on a generic draft and the notes will point at the same root problem every time: a hero whose
flaw isn't connected to their opponent, and a plot that doesn't force a self-revelation. Fix the
**opponent→weakness bond** first — redesign the antagonist to be the perfect scalpel for *this* hero's
specific flaw — and the organic structure starts assembling itself around that collision. That's the
implementation: you don't outline beats, you grow structure from the weakness–opponent bond outward.

## Trade-offs, when to use, and pitfalls

**When to reach for Truby.** When you want **depth, meaning, and originality** — character-driven
stories, tragedies, morally complex narratives, literary fiction, anything where "it hits the beats"
isn't enough. Also whenever a story feels *hollow despite being structurally correct*: that's almost
always a missing moral argument or a weak opponent–weakness bond, both of which are Truby's specialty.
And use the **character web** any time your supporting cast feels flat or interchangeable.

**When it's the wrong tool.** Truby's organic method is *harder and slower* than a beat sheet — it
demands you understand your story's moral core before you can structure it, which is a lot to ask under
a deadline or when you're a beginner who just needs *a* structure to finish something. For fast,
commercial, plot-first work, or for a first-timer who needs training wheels, Snyder's concreteness is
more immediately usable. The mature practice is to use both: Truby to find the moral spine and the web,
Snyder/McKee to check pacing and scene-level turns.

**Common pitfalls:**

- **A hero who already knows their need.** Kills the arc. The self-revelation must be a *discovery*;
  if the hero starts self-aware, there's nowhere to travel.
- **A generic opponent.** The single most common failure Truby targets: an antagonist who blocks the
  goal but doesn't attack the hero's *specific* weakness. Design the opponent *from* the weakness.
- **Preaching instead of arguing.** "Moral argument" does not mean a message stated aloud. If a
  character delivers the theme as a speech, you've broken it. The argument must be *dramatized* — proven
  by the plot and the self-revelation, never announced.
- **Designing characters in isolation.** A character built alone has no meaning; meaning comes from
  contrast. Always design the web — each character a different answer to the moral question.
- **Mistaking the 22 steps for a new template.** Ironically, you can turn Truby's own 22 steps into
  the very mechanical checklist he warns against. The 22 are a *guide to organic growth*, not a
  fill-in-the-blanks form. Keep the organic mindset.

## 🧠 Q&A Bank

**1. What is the core disagreement between Truby and Snyder?**
Mechanical vs organic form. Snyder imposes an external template (the 15-beat sheet) that's the same for
every story; Truby argues structure should *grow organically* from each story's own premise and the
hero's specific moral weakness, so every story's shape is unique and inevitable rather than stamped on.

**2. What are the two kinds of "need," and why must the hero not know their need at the start?**
Psychological need (what the hero must overcome *within* themselves) and moral need (how they must
change the way they *treat others*). The hero must not know it at the start because the entire story
exists to move them from *not seeing* their moral failing to *seeing* it (self-revelation). If they
already know, there's no arc to travel.

**3. What makes an opponent great in Truby's system — and what makes one generic?**
A great opponent competes for the same goal *and* is specifically designed to attack the hero's
particular moral weakness — a mirror and a scalpel that forces the flaw into the open toward the
self-revelation. A generic opponent merely blocks the goal (a standard bad guy) without targeting the
hero's specific weakness, so the collision reveals nothing about who the hero is.

**4. What does "story as moral argument" mean, and how is it different from preaching?**
It means the plot dramatizes a case about *how to live*: the hero pursues their goal the wrong way,
that way is tested against opponents embodying competing values, and the self-revelation delivers the
verdict — which *is* the theme. It differs from preaching because the argument is *proven by events*,
never announced in a speech. A character stating the theme aloud breaks it.

**5. What is the character web and four-corner opposition, and why never design a character alone?**
The character web is designing all major characters as a *system of contrasts*; four-corner opposition
places the hero and three opponents/allies at four corners, each embodying a different value, so the
story examines its moral question from four angles. You never design a character alone because a
character means something only in *contrast* to the others — meaning comes from difference.

**6. "What happens if…" — a story is structurally flawless but feels hollow and interchangeable. What
does Truby diagnose?**
A missing or weak moral core: no genuine moral argument, and/or an opponent who doesn't attack the
hero's specific weakness, and/or a supporting cast designed in isolation. The structure is correct but
disconnected from any *particular* meaning, which is exactly the disease of mechanical (template)
storytelling. The cure is to grow the structure from the weakness–opponent bond and a real character
web.

**7. How do the 7 key steps handle a tragedy, where the hero doesn't triumph?**
Seamlessly, because they're organic, not victory-shaped. In tragedy the self-revelation fails, comes
too late, or the hero refuses it, and the new equilibrium settles *lower* (as with Michael Corleone).
The same seven steps — weakness/need through new equilibrium — produce a redemption in one story and a
damnation in another, because their shape grows from the specific hero rather than a fixed template.

**8. Where did Truby's "organic" idea come from, and what's the seed of an organic story?**
From the Romantic critics' distinction (Coleridge and others, early 1800s) between mechanical form
(shape imposed from outside, like a cookie-cutter) and organic form (shape grown from within, like a
plant from a seed). In Truby, the seed is the premise plus the hero's moral weakness, and the
"designing principle" is the deep organizing logic unique to that particular story.

## 🛠️ Hands-on Exercise

Take a character-driven story you love (ideally one that *doesn't* fit a clean triumphant arc) and
reverse-engineer its organic spine: name the hero's **weakness** and their hidden **moral need**, their
conscious **desire**, and then — the key move — show *how the opponent is specifically designed to
attack that weakness.* Finally, state the **self-revelation** and show how it mirrors the weakness from
step 1. Then build a tiny **character web**: pick two other major characters and write, for each, the
competing *value* they embody relative to the hero. Hint: if the opponent could be swapped for a
generic villain without changing the hero's inner journey, you haven't found the real opponent — the
real one is inseparable from *this* hero's specific flaw.

## ✅ Recap

- Truby is the **anti-template** system: stories should grow **organically** from their own premise and
  the hero's specific **moral weakness**, not be stamped onto an external beat sheet — so every
  structure is unique.
- The **7 key steps** — weakness/need → desire → opponent → plan → battle → self-revelation → new
  equilibrium — are a mostly *internal, moral* spine whose shape differs per story. The arc runs from
  *not knowing* the need to *seeing* it (self-revelation).
- **Story is a moral argument**: the plot tests a way of living and the self-revelation is its verdict
  — theme *emerges*, it's never preached.
- The **opponent must be designed from the hero's weakness** (a mirror and scalpel), and characters
  must be built as a **web of contrasts** (four-corner opposition), never in isolation.
- Truby gives **depth and originality** but is harder and slower than Snyder; mature practice uses both
  — Truby for the moral spine and web, Snyder/McKee for pacing and scene-level turns.
